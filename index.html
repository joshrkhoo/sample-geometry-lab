From a data matrix to \(\bar{\mathbf x}\), \(\mathbf S\) and \(\mathbf R\)

Rows are observational units and columns are variables, so \(x_{jk}\) is variable \(k\) for unit \(j\). Each row is one point in the scatter plot. Drag a point, click empty space to add one, or double-click a point to remove it. You can also type into the table.

\[ \bar x_k=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n x_{jk} \qquad s_{ik}=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n(x_{ji}-\bar x_i)(x_{jk}-\bar x_k) \qquad r_{ik}=\frac{s_{ik}}{\sqrt{s_{ii}}\sqrt{s_{kk}}} \qquad \mathbf R=\mathbf D^{-1/2}\mathbf S\,\mathbf D^{-1/2} \]
Chapter 1 uses divisor \(n\) (written \(\mathbf S_n\)). R's cov() and Chapter 3's \(\mathbf S\) divide by \(n-1\). Switch between them below: \(\mathbf S\) changes, \(\mathbf R\) doesn't.

Three rulers: Euclidean, standardised and Mahalanobis

How far is the point \(P\) from the centre of the cloud? Each distance draws the set of points at the same distance as \(P\). Drag \(P\), reshape the cloud, or change the units of \(x_1\).

\[ d_E=\sqrt{(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)'(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)} \qquad d_S=\sqrt{\sum_k\frac{(x_k-y_k)^2}{s_{kk}}}=\sqrt{(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)'\mathbf D^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)} \qquad d_M=\sqrt{(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)'\mathbf S^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)} \]
Special cases: \(\mathbf S=\mathbf I\) gives \(d_M=d_E\), and a diagonal \(\mathbf S\) gives \(d_M=d_S\). Uses Ch 2's \(\mathbf S^{-1/2}\): \(d_M\) is the ordinary length after whitening.

  • Change units with \(k\): \(d_E\) changes, but \(d_S\) and \(d_M\) don't. A ruler that depends on the choice of units can't be trusted.
  • Along vs across: the two buttons put \(P\) at the same Euclidean distance. Across the cloud, \(d_M\) is much bigger. That point is far more unusual.

  • Push \(r\) toward \(\pm1\). \(\mathbf S\) nears singular and \(d_M\) across the cloud explodes. The formula needs \(\mathbf S^{-1}\).

Two pictures of one data matrix

An \(n\times p\) matrix \(\mathbf X\) is either n points in \(\mathbb R^p\) (rows) or p vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) (columns). With \(n=3,\ p=2\) the column picture fits in 3-D. Drag the points on the left.

Correlation is the cosine of an angle

AI Exploration-1, done by hand. \(\mathbf d_1,\mathbf d_2\in\mathbb R^5\) are centred (each sums to zero). Turn the angle and watch the five observations.

\[ r_{12}=\frac{\mathbf d_1'\mathbf d_2}{\|\mathbf d_1\|\,\|\mathbf d_2\|}=\cos\theta \qquad\text{and}\qquad |\mathbf d_1'\mathbf d_2|\le\|\mathbf d_1\|\,\|\mathbf d_2\| \ \Rightarrow\ -1\le r_{12}\le 1 \]

\(\bar{\mathbf x}\) and \(\mathbf S\) are random too

Before the data are collected, each row \(\mathbf X_j\) is a random vector, so \(\bar{\mathbf X}\) and \(\mathbf S\) are random as well. Draw many samples of size \(n\) from \(N_2(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) and check the theorem.

\[ E(\bar{\mathbf X})=\boldsymbol\mu \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\bar{\mathbf X})=\tfrac1n\boldsymbol\Sigma \qquad E(\mathbf S)=\boldsymbol\Sigma \qquad E(\mathbf S_n)=\tfrac{n-1}{n}\boldsymbol\Sigma \]
Uses Ch 2: linearity \(E(\mathbf A\mathbf X\mathbf B)=\mathbf A\,E(\mathbf X)\,\mathbf B\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\sum a_j\mathbf X_j)=\sum_j\sum_l a_ja_l\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf X_j,\mathbf X_l)\).

Why it works

\(E(\bar{\mathbf X})=\boldsymbol\mu\)
\[ E(\bar{\mathbf X})=E\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf X_j\Big)=\tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^nE(\mathbf X_j)=\tfrac1n\,n\boldsymbol\mu=\boldsymbol\mu \]
\(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{\mathbf X})=\boldsymbol\Sigma/n\)
\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\bar{\mathbf X})=\tfrac1{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{l=1}^n\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf X_j,\mathbf X_l)=\tfrac1{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^n\boldsymbol\Sigma=\tfrac1n\boldsymbol\Sigma \]

The rows are independent, so every cross term with \(j\ne l\) is zero.

Why divide by \(n-1\)?

Deviations are measured from \(\bar{\mathbf x}\), which is fitted to the same data. That makes the sum of squares too small on average:

\[ E\Big[\sum_{j}(\mathbf X_j-\bar{\mathbf X})(\mathbf X_j-\bar{\mathbf X})'\Big]=(n-1)\boldsymbol\Sigma \]

Dividing by \(n-1\) makes \(\mathbf S\) unbiased.

One number for spread: \(|\mathbf S|\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf S)\)

Both numbers compress the whole covariance matrix into one value. The ellipse is \((\mathbf x-\bar{\mathbf x})'\mathbf S^{-1}(\mathbf x-\bar{\mathbf x})=c^2\) with \(c=2\). The axes are fixed, so you can compare areas between settings.

\[ |\mathbf S| = s_{11}s_{22}(1-r_{12}^2)=\lambda_1\lambda_2 \qquad \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf S)=s_{11}+s_{22}=\lambda_1+\lambda_2 \qquad \text{area}=\pi c^2\sqrt{|\mathbf S|} \qquad |\mathbf S|=\frac{(\text{volume of }\mathbf d\text{'s})^2}{(n-1)^p} \]
Uses Ch 2: determinant \(|\mathbf A|=ad-bc\), and eigenvalues from \(|\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I|=0\). The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues and the trace is their sum.

  • Bigger variances raise \(|\mathbf S|\). Strong correlation of either sign pulls it toward 0.

The slide questions

Why is \(|\mathbf S|\ge 0\)?

\(\mathbf b'\mathbf S\mathbf b\) is the sample variance of the numbers \(\mathbf b'\mathbf x_j\), so it is \(\ge0\) for every \(\mathbf b\). That makes \(\mathbf S\) nonnegative definite (Ch 2), so \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\ge0\) and \(|\mathbf S|=\lambda_1\lambda_2\ge0\).

Why is \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf S)\ge0\)?

It is a sum of variances \(s_{ii}\ge0\). Equivalently, \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\ge0\).

When is \(|\mathbf S|=0\)?

Exactly when the deviation vectors are linearly dependent: one variable is a linear combination of the others, or \(n\le p\). Drag \(r\) to \(\pm1\) and the ellipse collapses to a line.

Inside \(\mathbf S\): rotate, stretch, rotate back

This tab brings in Chapter 2. The spectral decomposition shows what \(\mathbf S\) does geometrically. Its square root turns a circle into the ellipse from the previous tab, and its inverse square root turns the data back into a circle.

\[ \mathbf S=\mathbf P\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf P'=\lambda_1\mathbf e_1\mathbf e_1'+\lambda_2\mathbf e_2\mathbf e_2' \qquad \mathbf S^{1/2}=\mathbf P\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf P' \qquad \mathbf S^{-1/2}=\mathbf P\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1/2}\mathbf P' \qquad \mathbf R=\mathbf D^{-1/2}\mathbf S\,\mathbf D^{-1/2} \]
From Ch 2 (spectral decomposition, square-root matrices, \(\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf V^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^{-1/2}\)) and 3.3 (\(\mathbf R\) from \(\mathbf S\)). The covariance of \(\mathbf A\mathbf x\) is \(\mathbf A\mathbf S\mathbf A'\) (3.4), and that drives everything below.

Why this matters

  • Eigenvectors \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\) are the ellipse's axes. Eigenvalues are the variances along them.
  • If \(\mathbf z\) has covariance \(\mathbf I\), then \(\mathbf S^{1/2}\mathbf z\) has covariance \(\mathbf S^{1/2}\mathbf I\,\mathbf S^{1/2}=\mathbf S\). The Random sampling tab simulates data the same way (with a Cholesky factor, another square root of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\)).
  • Standardising fixes the scales but keeps the tilt, so its covariance is \(\mathbf R\). Whitening removes the tilt too, and its covariance is \(\mathbf I\).
  • The squared length after whitening, \(\|\mathbf S^{-1/2}(\mathbf x-\bar{\mathbf x})\|^2=(\mathbf x-\bar{\mathbf x})'\mathbf S^{-1}(\mathbf x-\bar{\mathbf x})\), is the statistical distance from the ellipse. It reappears in Chapter 4.

The bivariate normal: contours, probability ellipses and conditioning

The density is constant on the ellipses \((\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)=c^2\), the Mahalanobis circles from Chapter 1. Pick \(c^2\) from \(\chi^2_2\) and the ellipse holds a known probability. Drag the vertical slice to condition on \(X_1\).

\[ f(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{2\pi|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2}}e^{-\frac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)} \qquad (\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)\sim\chi^2_p \qquad X_2\mid X_1=x_1\ \sim\ N\!\Big(\mu_2+\tfrac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{11}}(x_1-\mu_1),\ \sigma_{22}-\tfrac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_{11}}\Big) \]
Axes of the ellipse: eigenvectors \(\mathbf e_i\) of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), half-lengths \(c\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) (Ch 2). For \(p=2\), \(\chi^2_2(\alpha)=-2\ln\alpha\) exactly. Conditional result: Theorem 4.6 with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_{12}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\) reduced to scalars.

What to notice

  • Move the slice: the conditional mean slides along the regression line, but the conditional variance stays the same. It doesn't depend on \(x_1\).
  • Conditioning shrinks the variance by the factor \(1-\rho^2\). Knowing \(X_1\) tells you more about \(X_2\) when \(|\rho|\) is large.
  • Set \(\rho=0\): the two curves coincide, so \(X_1\) tells you nothing about \(X_2\). For the normal, zero covariance means independence (Theorem 4.5).
  • About \(1-\alpha\) of the points land inside the ellipse. That is the \(\chi^2_2\) result, and the basis of the chi-square plot in the next tab.

Is it normal? Q-Q plots, \(r_Q\) and the chi-square plot

Pick a data set and run the lecture's checks on it. Drag any point in the scatter plot to make your own outlier, and watch where it lands in each plot.

\[ \text{Q-Q: plot } \Big(\Phi^{-1}\big(\tfrac{j-1/2}{n}\big),\ x_{(j)}\Big) \qquad r_Q=\operatorname{corr}\big(q_j,\,x_{(j)}\big) \qquad d_j^2=(\mathbf x_j-\bar{\mathbf x})'\mathbf S^{-1}(\mathbf x_j-\bar{\mathbf x})\ \dot\sim\ \chi^2_p \]
Reject normality at level \(\alpha\) if \(r_Q\) is below the critical point for \(n\) (Johnson & Wichern Table 4.2). For \(p=2\), \(\chi^2_2\) quantiles are \(-2\ln(1-\text{prob})\). About half the \(d_j^2\) should be below \(\chi^2_2(0.5)=1.39\).

Verdicts

Critical points at \(\alpha=0.05\), interpolated in \(n\).

Reading the plots

  • Straight line: consistent with normality. S-shape (steep at both ends): heavy tails. Bow: skewness.
  • One point far above the chi-square line: a multivariate outlier. Investigate it, don't just delete it.
  • Normal marginals are not enough. In the last data set \(Y=BZ\) with \(B=\pm1\), so both marginal Q-Q plots look perfect. The scatter is an X shape, though, and the chi-square plot bends. So does the Q-Q plot of \(x_1+x_2\), which piles up at zero.

Box-Cox: choosing a power to make data look normal

Powers below 1 pull in a long right tail, and powers above 1 stretch it out. Slide \(\lambda\) and watch the histogram and Q-Q plot. The likelihood curve underneath picks the best \(\lambda\) for you.

\[ x^{(\lambda)}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^\lambda-1}{\lambda}&\lambda\ne0\\[4pt] \ln x&\lambda=0\end{cases} \qquad \ell(\lambda)=-\frac n2\ln\Big[\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\big(x_j^{(\lambda)}-\overline{x^{(\lambda)}}\big)^2\Big]+(\lambda-1)\sum_{j=1}^n\ln x_j \]
\(\hat\lambda\) maximises \(\ell(\lambda)\). The last term is the Jacobian of the transformation. Without it, small \(\lambda\) would always win by shrinking the variance. The best \(\lambda\) often improves normality but doesn't guarantee it, so always re-check.

Notes

  • The radiation data (microwave ovens, door closed, \(n=42\)) give \(\hat\lambda\approx0.28\). The textbook rounds to \(\lambda=\tfrac14\), which is easier to interpret and almost as good.
  • The simulated sets are built so the answer is known. Log-normal has \(\ln x\) normal, so \(\hat\lambda\approx0\). Squared normal has \(\sqrt x\) normal, so \(\hat\lambda\approx\tfrac12\). Left-skewed has \(x^{1.5}\) normal, so \(\hat\lambda\approx1.5\).
  • \(\ell(\lambda)\) is flat near the top. Any \(\lambda\) close to \(\hat\lambda\) does about as well, so pick a convenient one.
  • Box-Cox needs \(x>0\). For counts, \(\sqrt x\) is the classic choice. For proportions, use \(\operatorname{logit}(\hat p)\). For correlations, use Fisher's \(z\).
